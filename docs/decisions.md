# Decisions

One entry per choice between options: what was decided, why, and what was rejected. Newest first. Earlier entries are reconstructed from code comments and project notes; dates are when the choice took effect.

### 2026-11-02: A reporting year's document is the one dated the year its period ends
The library dates a document by the year its period ends (the Source Library's convention): a fiscal year from April 2024 to March 2025 is 2025. The pipeline's reporting year counts that same year as 2024 (a year ending January to June is the year before). The reader took "the documents of 2024" literally, so for every company with a January-to-June year end it preferred the previous year's report and the model then took the previous year's column. Chosen: learn each company's year end from its documents (`period_end`, now in the evidence database; the first pages when it is missing), map the reporting year to the document year with that, and tell the model the year end in the words the report uses ("the year ended March 31, 2025", FY2025, FY24/25, in Japan FY2024), asking it for each column's period end and taking the reporting year from the period, not the heading. Rejected: re-dating the library's documents to reporting years (the Source Library and the analysts use the period-end year; the mapping belongs at the edge, where the question is asked); trusting the heading alone (a column headed "2024" is reporting year 2024 for a December year end and 2023 for a March one, so the heading cannot decide); asking the company's year end from the regulator filings only (only SEC and ESEF filers have them). The ORIX row that showed it was found by the owner reviewing restated rows.

### 2026-11-02: Element-level evidence is the ground truth for the Social and non-Social benchmarks
In 2025 the Climate team (ACT Core) verified each attribute; the Social and non-Social teams verified each element (Met, Partially met, Unmet) and left the attribute values unchecked. Chosen: score attribute readers only against ACT Core's attributes; for the other benchmarks, use the element rows (score, quotes, cited sources) to measure what the library holds and finds, and later the narrative readers, at element level. Rejected: using the Social and non-Social attribute values as right answers (no one confirmed them one by one); waiting for this year's attribute-level checks before measuring anything narrative (the element evidence already says which documents and pages answer each question).

### 2026-11-02: Every document the analysts saved comes into the library
The first Source Library import (October) brought only reports, and a code or human rights policy only when the library held none of that type, to keep the library to the documents the pipeline itself looks for. The element evidence of 2025 shows what the analysts actually answer from: sustainability reports 29%, codes and policies 30%, integrated and annual reports 21%, registration documents 2%, webpages 9%; and in a sample only 39 to 72% of the cited documents were in the library. Chosen: import every document type (not webpages: the library holds files), telling non-report documents apart by their file, and keep the current cycle's base in step every morning. Every imported row carries `origin` (Source Library 2024, 2025 or 2026): they measure completeness, not discovery, and every measure of the discovery must leave them out (the owner's rule, 2 Nov 2026). Rejected: importing webpages as documents (they are pages to monitor, a separate job); one document per company and type for policies (companies publish several and analysts cite different ones); waiting for the pipeline's own search to find them (the analysts already did, and those are the sources that answered last year).

### 2026-11-02: A sibling attribute's page points to the attribute's page
The owner's idea: when one attribute's figure is on page 28, a related attribute's figure is likely on page 28 or near it. Measured before building (331 company-years with validated answers for several attributes, documents of 108 pages in the median): Scope 1 and both Scope 2 figures on the same page 89 to 93% of the time and within two pages 92 to 96%; revenue and low-carbon capex on the same page 46%, within two pages 70%; across themes (emissions and employees, emissions and revenue) 10 to 25% on the same page, mostly the key-figures pages. Chosen: siblings only within a theme, declared in attributes.json, one direction (the easier attribute, Scope 1 or revenue, is read first and points to the harder one), and a page boost rather than a rule (the sibling's page is a strong hint, not proof). Rejected: siblings across themes (the shared key-figures page is already found by the labels); reading the whole family in one model call (a change of the reader's question and its checks, for later, when the narrative attributes come in groups).

### 2026-11-02: A model judges every cover the web search's rules pass
The rules of #173 were tested on synthetic covers; on the real files of the 34 audited companies they still passed 9 wrong documents of 20. Each kind needed a new rule (the subsidiary that names its parent without "subsidiary of", the forum's report on the company's own international domain, a magazine whose cover does not say magazine), and every wave brings new kinds. Chosen: keep the rules as a cheap first filter and ask the model, for each file they pass, the three questions an analyst asks (who publishes it, what it is, which period), passing it on only when all three fit. About 1,000 tokens per file with Haiku; a wave of 100 companies opens a few hundred files, a few cents. This reverses "rejected: asking a model to judge covers" in the entry below: the rules alone did not hold. Rejected: more rules only (one in four wrong after the first fix, almost half after the second on the hardest companies); the model alone without the rules (it would be asked about every search result, ten times the calls, and the rules settle the plain cases); a bigger model (the question is short and the cover text is clear).

### 2026-11-02: The web search opens every file before passing it on
Until 2 Nov a result on the company's own sites or a document host passed on its title and address alone; only results elsewhere were opened. An audit of the 167 documents the waves of 1 and 2 Nov brought in found 37 wrong (one in four): other companies' reports that name the company (Virgin Australia for Qatar Airways, JD Sports for New Balance, Johnson Outdoors for SC Johnson), local subsidiaries' accounts from a company registry (Lactalis Danmark A/S, Remondis A/S), foundations' and industry forums' reports, a university thesis, a rating agency's rationale, a second-party opinion, and files on the company's own sites that are not reports (a news release, a trend report, a whitepaper, fund reports, a quarterly). Chosen: every candidate is opened (own-site files too, at most 6 per family), and the checks grow with the distance from the company: on its own site the cover must show the company, a report title and a missing year; on a document host also its whole name on the title page; on an unrelated site also its name heading the file. On every site a file whose first lines say it is something else, whose first pages name another author or a parent, or that prints the company's name only inside a subsidiary's name (a country marker or a legal form of another country next to it) is refused. Sites that never hold the company's own report are not opened at all: governments, academic repositories, registries, foundations, .org domains, initiatives. A shared second-level domain (bank.in) and a folder of a bare government domain (michigan.gov/ors) count as the site, not the whole domain; a monitored page's domain that is the name plus a country (novonordisk-us, mccainindia) or a foreign country top-level domain (watsons.com.tr) is a local affiliate's, not the group's. Rejected: trusting own-site titles with a longer list of forbidden words (every wave brings new kinds); asking a model to judge covers (cost per file, and the rules are checkable); keeping the waves running while fixing (one in four wrong documents reaches analysts). Known limits: a listed subsidiary whose name contains the group's and no country word (Beijing Shougang Co. for Shougang), and a document that mentions the parent on its cover without the words "subsidiary of". Costs about one more download per document passed on; an own-site file that refuses the cloud runner is still passed from its title, for the inbox and the office runner to fetch.

### 2026-11-01: Pages next to a good page join the pool and score higher
The pool was the pages whose words match the attribute's labels, plus the pages the report's own index names and the ones carrying the company's remembered line. A table that runs on to the next page, or a figure on the page after the one the index names, carried no label and was never seen (the 1 Nov report counted "label on the next or previous page" among the misses). Chosen: fetch the page before and after each of the 10 best pages, score them like the rest, and add to every page a share of its best neighbour's score (NEIGHBOUR_WEIGHT 0.5; the whole share one page away, 0.4 two pages away). Rejected: giving the model page pairs for every candidate (doubles the tokens for every answer, most of which need one page); a decay over the whole document (what is 30 pages away is not more alike, and the scores would smear). The weights are first guesses, to be set by the evaluator; the shares are of the best score in the pool and taken from the scores before the step, so nothing travels further than two pages.

### 2026-11-01: Industry and region as fields on companies, loaded from the SDG2000 list
The reader's accuracy differs by country (India 41%, Japan 64%, China 88%) and industry (chemicals 47%, utilities 88%), and the next steps (scale rules by country, a memory shared by companies of one industry) need those facts on every company. Chosen: three text fields on `companies` (wba_industry, isic_code, hq_region) loaded by a re-runnable script from the list committed in `data/`. Rejected: a lookup to a new companies-master table (one more table to keep in step, for three values that change once a year); reading the CSV at run time in each script (the fields are then invisible in Airtable views and filters). The script never overwrites hq_country, ownership or isin: those may carry hand corrections.

### 2026-11-01: A record of what the model reader read, so a stopped run is carried on
The 1 Nov run (2022 to 2025, 8 shards) stopped at the 5-hour limit in every shard, and the reader always starts from the first company of its slice, so a second run would read the same companies again. Chosen: a `model_reads` table (company, attribute, year, when, found) and a skip of what was read in the last 30 days. Rejected: skipping years that already have a model reader row in attribute_values (a read that found nothing leaves no row, so those would be read again every run, and they are the costly ones); starting each run at a random company (no guarantee of covering the whole list). 30 days lets a run carry on for a month; `--fresh-days 0` reads everything again after a change to the reader.

### 2026-10-31: A Scope 2 figure with no basis goes under location-based, once
Many reports print one Scope 2 figure without saying location- or market-based. The reader proposed it under both attributes, and the reviewer rejected the market-based copy: we cannot know which it is. Chosen: file it under location-based only (the GHG Protocol's default method, and what a company reports when it buys no contractual instruments), say so in `detail`, and leave market-based empty. Rejected: under both (a duplicate a reviewer has to reject every time); under neither (loses a figure that is usually the location-based one). The reader report measures the cost on the market-based answers; if the validated answers file such figures under market-based too, the rule moves there. **Superseded 7 Nov 2026** (see below): they do, and the figure now goes under both, each saying the page names no basis.

### 2026-10-31: The report's own content index as the first signal that is not a label
The page ranking combined six signals from the page itself (label match, rule candidate, table-ness, year, label hits, document weight), and the pages it missed mostly carried no label at all. The GRI and ESRS content index, and the table of contents, are other pages of the same report saying where the figure is, the way early web search read the text of the links pointing at a page. The index rows are matched on the attribute's disclosure codes and words from `attributes.json`, the printed pages they cite are mapped to PDF pages with an offset estimated from the document's own page numbers, and those pages join the pool with a fixed boost. Chosen over learning the weights from the validated answers (next, once the reviewers' decisions add to them) and over reading the index with a model (a regular table; rules read it for nothing). Rejected: trusting the printed page number as the PDF page (covers and inside pages shift it by 0 to 4 pages).

### 2026-10-31: One model call per company, attribute and report, for every year it prints
The model reader asked for one year per call, although the table it reads prints two to five years side by side, so the history cost one call per year (a 2024 and 2025 run on 167 companies took five hours and $1). The model now returns every reporting year the pages print, each entry checked on its own page and against its column heading (a "2023" column cannot be reporting year 2024; "FY25" may be 2025 or 2024). Years are read newest first, and a year already returned as a comparison column is not read again. What is given up: the figure for 2024 may come from the 2025 report's comparison column rather than the 2024 report, and a restated figure differs from the one first reported (the validated answers are usually the latter); `detail` says which report it came from, and the Generic reader report measures how often those comparison figures match the validated answers, which decides whether a direct read of earlier years is worth paying for. Measured on the 31 Oct 2026 run (4,044 model rows, 809 with a validated answer): a figure read from the year's own documents matched 76% of the time (186 of 245), a comparison figure from the next year's report 66% (317 of 478), from two years later 61% (48 of 79). Every version is kept, not only the one shown: in the evidence database (`value_versions`), not in attribute_values, so the analysts review one value per company, attribute and year and see the others as a note (`other_reports`, `restated`). A company with five different Scope 1 figures for 2023 says something about its reporting, and the table lets that be measured. Rejected: one attribute_values row per version (four or five times the rows for the analysts, and the Disclosure Library's 500,000-record cap), and the existing `datapoints` table (meant for the one final value, with no deterministic key). So since 1 Nov 2026 every year asked is read and its own figure wins; comparison figures are kept for years not asked or where the year's read finds nothing. The cost is about one more call per company, attribute and year asked. Rejected: one call for all attributes of a company at once, because retrieval is per attribute (different pages hold emissions and revenue) and a longer reply is cut more often.

### 2026-10-31: Parallel jobs for the model reader, each a fixed slice of the companies
All 2,000 companies take about 25 hours of reading at one call per answer. The run can be split into up to 8 jobs that each take a fixed slice of the companies (by a hash of the id, so a company always lands in the same slice), write their own rows (ids are deterministic, so no duplicates) and pace their writes so that together they stay within the base's five requests a second. Rejected: one job over several days (GitHub's six-hour limit) and a queue in the database (more moving parts than a slice).

### 2026-10-31: Stock exchanges as a gap filler, Hong Kong and mainland China first
About 1 in 5 companies block automated access to their sites, many of them Chinese, and the analysts found reports (Transsion) only on an exchange. Listed companies must file their annual report with the exchange, whose databases are open: HKEXnews and cninfo (Shanghai and Shenzhen) first, because they cover the largest blocked group. Same rule as the SEC and ESEF: added only for a fiscal year with no annual report of the company's own in the library, so the record count stays low. Rejected for now: Japan (EDINET, an API key), Korea (DART, a key), India (BSE); next in line if these two work.

### 2026-10-30: Regulator filings are read as web pages, not printed to PDF
The 655 SEC and ESEF filings in report_library had no text. Printing them to PDF (as capture_pages.py does for web pages) was rejected: a 10-K prints to roughly 5 to 15 MB, and a file we make ourselves can only reach Airtable through the direct upload, which takes 5 MB at most (files Airtable copies from a public address can be far bigger, which is why larger reports are attached). The filing's own HTML marks every printed page, so `web_filing.py` splits it there and the text store gets the same pages the SEC or ESEF viewer shows; the row keeps the official address and no file.

### 2026-10-30: No learned vocabulary in retrieval (#145 closed)
`vocabulary.json` (#142) added a second search with the 12 strongest learned terms per attribute, meant to reach pages that print the value with no label. Scored on the same 200 answers, the share with the number on the ranked pages barely moved (revenue 55 to 57 %, Scope 1 72 %, Scope 2 location 42 %, market 50 to 48 %, capex 25 %), and the scoring run took twice as long. Not merged; the missed capex pages need another approach (the EU Taxonomy tables split the figure over rows).

### 2026-10-30: Revenue from a filing is its largest revenue concept
The revenue concepts were tried in a fixed order (`us-gaap:Revenues`, then contract revenue excluding and including assessed tax, net sales, revenue net of interest expense). In the first analyst review of `attribute_values`, 4 of the 5 SEC rows checked were rejected because the order picked a narrower line than WBA's revenue: net sales after excise taxes, contract revenue without other revenue, a card issuer's contract revenue instead of its revenue net of interest expense. The narrower concepts are always parts of the total, so `sec_facts.choose` now takes the largest the filing reports for the year (each concept as first filed; equal values keep the order). Applies to ESEF too, which uses the same choice. Rejected: reordering the list, because the right total sits in a different concept from one company to the next. Not solved by this: a total printed only in a company's own extension concept (gross sales before royalties, total revenue including other revenue in some IFRS reports, revenue including joint ventures).

### 2026-10-23: A browser on the owner's computer for refused sites
Plain requests from the owner's computer reached 11 of 30 refused recent reports; most of the rest check for a real browser. The owner decided to try a second pass in Microsoft Edge without a window (the base is a prototype and the reports are public). Safeguards: paths that robots.txt disallows are skipped; nothing clicks, types or solves, so captchas and logins stay refused; one page at a time with a pause; it runs only on the owner's computer. Rejected: solving captchas or using captcha services; paid proxies or rotating addresses.

### 2026-10-23: Refused files are fetched from the owner's computer
Sites that refuse our robot do so by network address: from GitHub's cloud servers a browser-like request and Internet Archive copies (also at other addresses of the same file) recovered 0 of 61 recent sustainability and annual reports (#53, closed without merging). The robot therefore also runs on the owner's Windows computer as a GitHub self-hosted runner, attached only to the private repository wba-text-store, where the workflow and the Airtable token live; it runs this repository's `pc_fetch.py` from main every 4 hours, every day (the owner's choice: the computer may be off; at most one run waits), up to 300 refused files and 150 refused report pages a run, 90 minutes. Reading the refused pages extends it beyond the 3,000 known files: reports on the roughly 340 sites that refuse us entirely become visible. A second computer can join with its own runner. Approved by the owner on 23 Oct. Rejected: attaching the runner to this public repository (any pull request could run code on the computer); paid proxies or rotating residential addresses (getting around a refusal rather than reading as an ordinary visitor would); running the runner as a Windows service (the service account does not see the user's Python).

### 2026-10-23: Disclosure completeness and page completeness, separately
The six completeness checks mix two different things: what the company discloses and we hold (four document checks) and whether we watch its report pages (two page checks, the health of our collection). A company can hold every report while we lose its page, and the other way round. Two scores now sit beside the combined one: `disclosure_completeness` and `page_completeness`, with daily averages in `base_health`. The combined `completeness` is kept unchanged so its history stays comparable. Suggested by the owner. Rejected: replacing `completeness` with the disclosure score (breaks the trend line and every view built on it).

### 2026-10-23: Page checks count only live pages (completeness rules v3, approved)
Under v2 the report hub and sustainability page checks pass when the company has any page of that type, even one unticked (monitoring switched off as junk) or gone (404/410). That flatters completeness: a company whose only reports page disappeared still shows the check as passed. v3 counts only pages still watched that have not gone. Measured with `company_coverage.py --compare --compare-rules v2,v3` on the pull request; measured on 23 Oct: average completeness 79.5% to 78.4%, 80 companies lose the report hub check and 52 the sustainability page check, the disclosure checks do not change. Approved by the owner the same evening and switched on (COVERAGE_RULES=v2 restores the old page checks). Gone pages are replaced nightly since #54, so most companies keep the check through the replacement. Rejected: counting blocked pages (403) as failed (the page is right, only our robot is refused).

### 2026-10-23: Non-English documents typed from their cover title
About 30,000 documents had no type: the Airtable AI field types English documents only, and their address said nothing. They did not count for completeness. A dictionary of cover-title phrases in a dozen languages (`cover_terms.json`: Portuguese, Spanish, French, German, Italian, Dutch, Swedish, Danish, Norwegian, Finnish, Polish, Turkish, Indonesian, Russian, Japanese, Chinese, Korean, Thai, Vietnamese, Arabic) now types them when the address and the file name say nothing; the phrase that appears first on the cover wins (the title comes first). Measured on the pull request by `year_eval.py` against the sure true types of non-English documents (mostly the analysts' Source Library labels). Rejected: switching the Airtable AI field on for every language (about 30,000 AI runs, an Airtable credit cost, and its accuracy on these languages unmeasured); machine translation of every cover (cost, and the title phrase is what decides).

### 2026-10-23: Clear page matches are added without a person
Suggested pages waited in `suggested_links` until a person ticked `approve_suggestions`, and report pages that started answering 404 stayed monitored, read every night for nothing. Nobody reviews suggestions reliably, so `discover_pages.py` now adds a candidate on its own when it is a clear best match: the only candidate of its type with 3 or more document links, or one with at least twice the document links of the next. It goes through the same path as an approval (`inbox.py`: deduplicated, deleted pages never come back, scanned within minutes). Gone pages are searched for first; the gone page is unticked with a dated note saying what replaced it. Ambiguous candidates still wait as suggestions. Rejected: adding every candidate (more records and wrong pages harvesting the wrong documents); keeping approval by a person (it does not happen). Found on the way: the page checks of completeness count a page of the type even when it is unticked or gone; to be measured and fixed separately.

### 2026-10-23: Claude labels the daily cards; the owner checks about 5
The daily cards asked the owner to classify documents from scratch into 24 types. He is not a document-type specialist and asked Claude about almost every card, so his labels added little the pre-read had not already said. Now Claude (Opus, the strongest model) labels every card with a confidence level and the quoted evidence; the owner checks only the cards where a person adds something: low or medium confidence, high-stakes disagreements with the pipeline, and 2 random high-confidence cards a day that measure Claude itself. One click per card (Agree, Disagree, Can't tell); cards left unanswered for 3 days settle with Claude's answer, so nothing depends on the owner's time. Labels keep `labelled_by` (Edi, Claude, source_library, correction), and Claude's labels are never used to score the Airtable AI type, to avoid one model grading another; the Source Library 2025 labels remain the independent truth for that. The run counts against the owner's Claude plan, not extra spend. Rejected: keeping the owner as the first labeller (slow, and less accurate than the model on type distinctions); letting Claude label without any human audit (its error rate would be unknown).

### 2026-10-23: The owner does not work through failure rows
Pipeline QA held about 36,000 open failures on its first day, with one person to act. The first design said a person acts on PAGE_BLOCKED, PAGE_GONE, PAGE_WRONG_TYPE and unresolved CO_GAP rows; the owner pointed out that this would never happen at that scale. Now: the weekly review fixes gone and wrongly typed pages itself in the Disclosure Library (new address into `add_link`, page `type`, `monitor`), the daily gap research works on CO_GAP, the robot retries blocked sites, and the owner acts only on analyst flags and on at most 5 items a week the Monday notification lists ("Para ti esta semana"). `who_fixes` says "Not you" on every other code. Card instructions changed the same day: `true_type` and `true_year` on every card (the year measurement counts only cards with `true_year`), `verdict` optional. Every code now has an automatic owner, a script or a scheduled Claude task; where it is not built yet (blocked sites, scanned PDFs, non-English typing, failed-job re-runs, gone-page replacement), the gap is listed in quality.md and the failures stay counted until it is. Analyst flags are a bonus signal; nothing depends on analysts reviewing. Rejected: a daily triage of Open failures by the owner (one person cannot keep up, and most rows are closed by one code fix); relying on analysts to review (they may not).

### 2026-10-23: Completeness rules v2, annual reports that carry the sustainability disclosure
Two gaps in the checks. (1) The type "Registration Document" holds 2,746 documents: 1,157 under 20 pages, almost all bond final terms, pricing supplements and prospectus supplements, but also the universal registration documents that ARE the annual report in France, Spain and Belgium (AXA, Sodexo, TotalEnergies, Eiffage, BBVA); none counted for the annual check. (2) Since CSRD, BRSR and the Hong Kong ESG rules, many companies publish the sustainability disclosure inside the annual report; 117 of the 530 companies without a 2024+ sustainability report hold a 2024+ whole annual report, 10-K or URD classed E embedded. v2: a URD (title names a registration document or annual (financial) report, 100+ pages, no prospectus or debt words) passes the annual check; a whole annual report, 10-K or URD classed E embedded passes the sustainability check when its sustainability section is a run of 8+ pages or its contents name a sustainability statement or BRSR (approved by the owner on 23 Oct after the hand check: blocks of 3-7 pages, e.g. a CSR chapter, did not amount to reporting sustainability). `company_coverage.py --compare` measures it (average, companies that change, the documents that make them change, a hand-check sample); Approved by the owner on 23 Oct with the 8-page threshold; v2 is the default from #49 (COVERAGE_RULES=v1 restores the old checks), and `companies.sust_reporting` says dedicated / embedded / both / none. Rejected: adding the whole Registration Document type to the annual family (bond documents would pass the check); counting any annual report for sustainability (financial-only reports would pass); retyping annual reports with embedded sections as Integrated Report (the type is the analysts' label and must stay comparable with the Source Library).

### 2026-10-23: House rules for human rights documents
A modern slavery statement (UK and Australian Modern Slavery Acts; also "human rights and modern slavery statement", "slavery and human trafficking statement") is an annual report on forced-labour risk in operations and supply chain, not a policy: type **Social Reports**. A human rights statement or commitment without modern slavery ("Statement on Human Rights") is the company's human rights policy under another name: **Human Rights Policy**. A "modern slavery policy" stays Human Rights Policy. Reason: the Human Rights Policy check asks whether the company has a human rights commitment; a statement required by law would otherwise pass it. The analysts' short type list put most of these under Policy Documents; `sl_labels.py` applies the house rules to the labels it writes (with the rule in `reason`) and never writes to the Source Library. Rejected: following the analysts' Policy Documents (a catch-all that hides what the document is); counting modern slavery statements as Human Rights Policy (inflates the check).

### 2026-10-23: Files too big to attach are read from the company's address
Airtable attaches a file either by fetching its URL itself (many company sites refuse Airtable's servers) or through the upload endpoint, which takes at most 5 MB. A PDF over 5 MB that Airtable could not fetch got `file_status = too_big` and was never read: no first pages, no type, no year, no full text. On 23 October that was 2,996 rows, 1,690 of them untyped and 1,221 dated 2023+, across 215 companies; the biggest files are the full annual, integrated and sustainability reports (CLP's 256-page 2025 annual report showed as "Document 2025"). `build_cover_text.py` and `build_fulltext.py` now read such rows straight from `document_url` (up to 150 MB), so they get first pages, AI type, year and full text like any other; only the attachment stays empty (open the link instead). Rejected: compressing or splitting PDFs to fit 5 MB (changes the file, loses pages); hosting copies elsewhere for Airtable to fetch (storage, and a public copy of every report).

### 2026-10-23: Year rule changes are measured before they are written
On 22 October two bulk rewrites of document years (first-pages fiscal years for 41,697 documents; the doc_year rules over the whole library) ran between two nightly completeness runs, and average completeness fell from 80.0% to 78.9% the next morning: some of the new years were corrections, some were new errors (a 2024 quarterly release dated 2023 because it quotes the previous Form 10-K; a 2024 annual report excerpt dated 2015 from a ten-year table). Three rules changed: a comparison period ("the preceding quarter ended ...") is not evidence; a title on the first pages two or more years away from the decided year costs 30 points (a report is titled by its period or the year after, never two years apart), so the medium-confidence text year drops below 60 and the title rule decides; a run of three or more years at the top of a cover is a table header, not a title. Every change to the year rules now runs `year_eval.py` on the pull request (main vs branch against every sure true year) and is merged only when it fixes more than it breaks; bulk rewrites follow the merge, not the other way round. Measured on the PR: the stored years scored 83.0% against 3,986 true years and the rules on main 83.4%, so the 22 October rewrites were net corrections and the completeness drop was mostly real. A first version also ignored periods quoted from another document ("our Annual Report on Form 10-K for the year ended ..."): it broke 11 years and fixed 3, because in US sustainability reports, CDP answers and proxy statements the 10-K cited is the reporting year itself; dropped. Rejected: letting any title year override the text (sustainability reports are often titled one year after the period they cover); reverting the rewrites (most of the changed years were corrections).

### 2026-10-22: A file named "financial statements" can be the whole annual report
In Italy ("Relazione finanziaria annuale"), Germany ("Jahresabschluss und Lagebericht") and the Nordics the statutory annual report is published under the name of its financial statements, but holds the management report, the corporate governance report and, from 2025, the sustainability statement (Acea 2025: 452 pages, file "2025-consolidated-financial-statements.pdf"). `doc_scope.py` now keeps such a file Full when its first pages list both a management report and the financial statements and the contents page numbers reach 70 to 105% of the file's length. Tested on the 487 "Part of a report" rows of 60+ pages: 7 change, all whole reports. Rejected: counting the parts named on the first pages without the page test (46 changes, most of them wrong: the sections of split UK reports carry the whole report's navigation bar); a page-count floor such as 150 pages (standalone financial statements volumes of banks run to 400 pages and are real parts).

### 2026-10-23: Sustainability class for documents the full text never reaches

### 2026-10-22: Where the sustainability disclosure lives: a class next to the type
Since the 2024 reports many companies put the sustainability disclosure inside the annual report (EU CSRD sustainability statements, India's BRSR, Hong Kong ESG sections, UK TCFD chapters) and stopped the separate report. ## 2026-10-26 How the evidence database is filled
The text store (private repository, gzipped page files) stays the source; Supabase is a copy that can be rebuilt from it. `load_supabase.py` loads only files it has not loaded before (table `load_log`), oldest first, so a run can stop anywhere and the next continues. For each document only its newest text is copied, and its earlier pages are replaced, because a re-extracted document may have fewer pages; this replaces derived rows in the copy and deletes nothing in Airtable or the store. Every document the store has tried gets a `documents` row, failures included (text_status), so coverage gaps are visible in the database too. The word index uses Postgres's 'simple' configuration (no stemming): the corpus is in many languages, and figures, acronyms and names must match as written; it reads the first 200,000 characters of a page and is built once after the first big load, which is much faster than updating it row by row. Extracted values get their own table (`datapoints`) with the value as printed and normalized, unit, period, scope, document, page, quote, and whether it was stated or calculated (the golden dataset holds calculated answers, such as a board share worked out from "1 of 12"). Loading stops at SUPABASE_MAX_GB (repository variable, 6 GB when unset): a Supabase disk that fills puts the database in read-only mode, and automatic disk growth is too slow for a bulk load. Rejected: loading from Airtable (no full text there), stemmed English search (misses other languages), and building the index before the load.

## 2026-10-26 File metadata as a clue to the publication date, not as the date
Owner's question: do we look at the metadata of the files? Every PDF carries a creation and a modification date. They are recorded (`file_dates.py`, fields file_created, file_modified, file_dates_from) for the key report types from 2023, but they are noisy: the creation date is the day the final file was exported, usually days or weeks before the release, and the modification date moves whenever the file is re-saved or replaced, sometimes years later. So they will rank below every date the report states (regulator filing, a stated publication date, board approval) and are accepted only when they fall after the period end and before the robot first saw the file; they also serve to flag a stated date that is far from the file's own dates. Rejected: taking the creation date as the publication date (export, not release), and the web server's Last-Modified header (often the upload or the last server migration, and the attachment host does not give the company's).

## 2026-10-26 Searching the evidence database: through functions, WBA addresses only

Searches use the same 'simple' word index as the load (no stemming, every language, phrases in quotes, -word to exclude). Rejected: opening the tables to signed-in users with row level security policies (any column and any query, including ones that scan millions of pages), and a domain allow-list in Supabase's sign-up settings alone (it does not protect the data if a sign-up slips through). ## 2026-10-26 Phrase search: store the words, put the filters in the index
The first search functions (same day) re-read each candidate page's text to check a phrase, because the word index holds words but not their positions, and Postgres recomputes the words from the text for the check. For "scope 3" that is every page with both "scope" and "3": hundreds of thousands, at about 0.4 ms each, so the count ran past two minutes. Measured on a 1-in-11 copy of the corpus (607,081 pages) with the same memory settings as the Small compute: "scope 3" count 11.8 s, results 2.7 s; at full size about 11 times that. Chosen: keep each page's words with positions in a column (`pages.words`, the textbook Postgres layout for this), so the phrase check reads them instead of re-reading the text (0.05 ms a page): count 1.6 s, results 0.6 s on the copy, cold cache. And since every page fetched that a filter then throws away costs a disk read on a 2 GB machine, the document's year, type and company are copied onto the page and indexed together with the words (one GIN index with btree_gin): a search for "climate" in 2025 annual reports fetches only pages that match all three, 5 ms warm. Newest first is then a loop over years from the newest, stopping when enough rows are found, so a rare phrase and a common word cost about the same; counts stop at 20,000 pages. The copies are kept in step by two triggers (a page written gets its document's values; a document whose year, type or company changes updates its pages), so no writer can leave them stale. Cost: the words take about twice the space of the text (positions compress badly), about 21 GB at full size; the index with the three extra columns is 2 to 4 percent larger than one on the words alone. The disk goes to 100 GB, which the pre-2021 corpus needs anyway. The migration runs inside the 6-hourly load, in batches that continue across runs, and the search functions switch to the new index only when it exists, so the search keeps working on the old index meanwhile. Rejected: a lossless index with positions (RUM) and the PGroonga engine, both available on Supabase but neither testable here before touching the paid database; keeping the text-only index and bounding candidates (a common word in a phrase defeats any bound); sorting all matches by year (reads every match before showing one).

## 2026-10-26 Measure the evidence layer before any extraction model
The extraction pilot starts with a measurement that needs no model: for every validated 2025 answer of a pilot attribute, is the page the analyst cited among the top pages our search returns for that attribute? The validated answers (the golden dataset, read only) carry the quote, the page and the source document, so three things can be measured separately: whether we hold the cited document and its text (collection), whether the analyst's quote is in our text (extraction: a quote found nowhere means a scan, a different file or a garbled text), and whether the search words rank the cited page high (retrieval). Whatever model reads the passages later cannot be right about a page that is not among them, so this is the floor. The analyst's page number is used only as a fallback, with two pages of play, because it is often the printed folio and not the PDF page; the quote itself, as a six-word phrase searched inside the document, locates the page in our numbering. Pilot attributes: the three ITA attributes that already have a code (board climate expertise, climate policies supported, low-carbon capex) and three with many validated answers (Scope 1, Scope 2, revenue). Of 480 validated low-carbon capex answers only 29 carry a quote, so attributes with many quotes weigh more in the measurement. Owner's rule: the approach must not be tailored attribute by attribute, so that a change of methodology or of the attributes can be answered quickly. ## The model reads; code computes and checks (29 Oct 2026)
The rule reader found the right value for about 10 % of the validated answers, while retrieval had put the number on the ranked pages for 32 to 60 %: the loss is in reading flattened tables. ## The company's memory: the line of another year's right answer (1 Nov 2026)

Companies print a figure on the same line from one report to the next. When the right value for another year is known (a validated answer now; accepted rows later), the page of that year's documents that prints it shows the line, the document type and the scale. `company_memory.py` keeps those for the nearest other year and uses them two ways: this year's pages that carry 80% of the line's words join the pool and get 1.0 on the page score, and the model reader is told how the company printed the figure that year. Not the value: the reader would anchor on it, and the year's own column is what is wanted. The year being read is never used, so the Generic reader's score stays honest. Chosen before learning general ranking weights from the right answers, because it needs no training set and the signal is the strongest we have per company; general learning from the reviewers' decisions comes next. First score (1 Nov 2026, 40 answers per attribute): revenue with a memory 9 of 9 on the ranked pages and 56% right against 61% and 45% without; capex 0 of 10 right with one (the line of another year's capex figure is rarely the line of this year's), so `"memory": false` switches it off per attribute. Scope 1, Scope 2 and employees got no memory: the validated answers hold one year for them. Their memory will come from accepted rows and from matched rows in `value_versions`.

The search index (`pages_search` on words, year, doc_type, wba_id) is not extended with library_id for this. The memory looks up pages by document and page number, which the primary key already covers, and searches within the company's documents by restricting to them first (as the capex query does since 31 Oct 2026). Rebuilding the index on every page of the store takes hours on Supabase (27 Oct 2026) and would not make these lookups faster.

## Web search for missing reports: Serper, on the company's own sites (1 Nov 2026)

When a company's report is not linked from the pages we watch, or its site refuses us, and no stock exchange filing has it, nothing brought it in. A web search does. Google's own Custom Search JSON API takes no new customers since 20 January 2026 and stops on 1 January 2027; what Google offers instead is limited to 50 domains (Programmable Search Element, Agent Search) or returns written answers, not results (grounding). Serper returns Google's results, takes `site:` and `filetype:pdf`, and costs about $1 per 1,000 searches ($50 minimum, credits expire after six months). It is unofficial (it reads Google's result pages; Google's case against SerpApi, a similar service, was dismissed in July 2026), so the provider sits behind one class (`Serper` in `web_search.py`) and can be swapped; Brave Search API ($5 per 1,000, its own index) is the fallback.

A result on the company's own sites (or a known document host, with the company's name) that names the report and a missing year is added on sight. Any other result that could be the report is opened and judged on its first pages: the company's name anywhere in the first 15 pages or the file's properties (a cover may carry only a logo), the report and a missing year in the first pages (deeper pages print every comparison year), and 8 pages or more. A search by name also finds news sites, aggregators and companies with similar names, so the address alone cannot be trusted, but a company's report on a CDN, a group site or an archive must not be lost either (the owner's call, 1 Nov 2026); the cover settles it. Up to 4 files are opened per company and family; what fails is listed in `web_search_note` with the reason. Documents go through `research_link` and the inbox, not straight into report_library, so the duplicate and tombstone guards, the attachment and the typing are the ones every other document gets. Rejected: a monthly sweep of every company (it searches companies that have their reports and spends credits for nothing), and adding results from any site.

## Page ranking: measure BM25 and a learned ranker offline before touching the readers (4 Nov 2026)
What: `rank_eval.py` compares, on the gold answers, today's `ts_rank_cd` with Okapi BM25 and with a logistic regression over page features, and changes nothing in production. If one wins clearly, a later pull request moves it into `extract_eval.ranked_pages` and the narrative reader, and the reader evaluation is run again.
Why: `ts_rank_cd` counts how often and how close the query words are, but not how rare they are, so "emissions" on a strategy page weighs as much as "tCO2e" in a table; and it does not normalise for page length. BM25 adds both. The analysts' cited pages are relevance judgements we already hold (a few thousand), so a ranker can learn which page features matter instead of the hand-set weights of `page_score`.
How it is kept honest: the learned ranker never scores a company it was trained on (five folds split by company); only answers whose quote is found in our text count, so the cited page is known in our numbering; and the "best of the alternative queries" figure that gold_eval reports is shown next to a single ranking, since picking the luckiest query after the fact flatters it.
Rejected for now: computing BM25 inside Postgres (needs an extension or a per-word document frequency table to maintain; first see whether it is worth it); a pairwise or gradient-boosted ranker and scikit-learn (a plain logistic regression shows whether the features carry signal, needs no new dependency, and is easy to port); document frequencies over the whole table (one count per word over millions of pages; a 1% repeatable sample gives the same ordering of words).

Update 4 Nov 2026, after the full run (2,251 usable answers): cited page in the top 3 for 44% of answers with today's search as one query, 62% with BM25, 66% with the learned ranker, and 80% with BM25 best of the alternative queries. Best-of uses hindsight, so the experiment now adds reciprocal rank fusion of the per-query lists, the way to get most of that 80% in one list a reader can use. Fusion was chosen over picking one query per attribute (the best query differs by company wording) and over concatenating the queries (that is BM25 single, which loses phrase-level wording).

Update 4 Nov 2026, readers: the narrative reader takes its pages from BM25 over any of the query words (`page_ranker.py`, #207), since it had no numeric check to fall back on and 70 to 95 answers per attribute found no pages with all words required. The generic reader got BM25 as an option only (`--text-rank bm25`, #208), default still ts_rank_cd, because its page score mixes the text rank with number and label checks and the gain has to be measured on its own scoreboard first. Rejected for now: the learned ranker in either reader (four points over BM25 in the experiment, and it needs features computed on every candidate page). Measured 5 Nov 2026 (full runs, 100-minute budget, about 66 answers per attribute, same answers for both): the validated number is on the reader's ranked pages less often with BM25 than with ts_rank_cd for every attribute (Scope 1 74% to 62%, market-based Scope 2 73% to 55%, employees 72% to 55%, revenue 76% to 77%, low-carbon capex 49% to 43%), and right answers fell on each (Scope 1 14% to 9%). So the generic reader keeps ts_rank_cd. Likely why: the reader ranks only the year's own documents and needs every query word on the page (the label and the unit together), which ts_rank_cd's AND query gives and BM25's any-word query loosens; the ranking experiment's gain (45% to 63% top 3) came from finding the right page across all of a company's pages, which is the narrative reader's problem, not this one. Measured 5 Nov 2026 (ACT, 671 answers, $0.37): answers with no pages fell from 70 to 95 per attribute to 26 to 42, and the sustainability governance attribute rose to 80% right (recall 82%), but the climate one fell to 58% (the model said yes on 18 of 223, recall 13%) and the multi-select to 39% exact: BM25 alone ranked general sustainability pages above the climate-specific ones. So the narrative reader now fuses both rankings (reciprocal rank fusion of the strict ts_rank_cd list and the BM25 list). Correction the same day: that switch, like the BM25 one before it, was made without a side-by-side run, and BM25 had been made the default on the strength of the ranking experiment alone. The narrative reader is back on the strict ts search by default; bm25 and fused are options (`--pages`), and a default changes only after a run on the same answers shows no attribute more than 3 points worse (house rule in CLAUDE.md). Rejected: keeping the newest method as default while it is measured, because every run in between would be scored on an unproven search. Side by side on the same 671 ACT answers (5 Nov 2026): strict ts 78% / 79% / 52% exact; BM25 80% / 58% / 39%; fused 77% / 79% / 46%. Fused finds pages for far more answers (no pages 21 to 26 per attribute against 69 to 93) but is 6 points worse on the multi-select, so by the rule the strict search stays the default. More pages did not mean more right answers: where the strict search finds nothing, the answer is usually no, which it already gets right.

## Near-duplicates: exact page fingerprints within a company, not MinHash (4 Nov 2026)
What: `dup_audit.py` compares documents of one company by the fingerprints of their pages (the words of a page, punctuation and spacing ignored). Why: the same report reaches the library by several routes (company site, CDN, Source Library, web search, filings) and sections are published on their own; copies are read and counted twice. Within one company there are tens of documents, so an exact comparison through an inverted index is cheap, and it is stricter than MinHash: a page where one figure changed stays different, which matters for restated numbers. Rejected: MinHash over word shingles across the whole library (finds near-identical text across companies, which is mostly boilerplate; costly in plain Python for no gain here); comparing titles or addresses (the copies are found precisely because those differ).

Zones (4 Nov 2026): pages in the evidence database are plain text with line breaks, no layout, so zones are read from the lines themselves (a short line with numbers is a table row, a short line with no closing punctuation is a heading). Chosen over re-extracting layout from the PDFs (a new text build of every document) to see first whether zones help at all. BM25F (weight the frequencies, saturate once) rather than adding separate per-zone BM25 scores, which lets one zone dominate. The weights are set by hand for this first test; if zones help, the learned ranker's zone features say how much each zone is worth.

Query expansion (4 Nov 2026): the added words come from the analysts' cited pages (relevance feedback from known answers), scored by how well they single out the cited page inside its own document rather than how often they appear, so words on every page of a report (company, group, year) score nothing. Learned within the company folds of the learned ranker. Not split by language yet: the gold answers with a located cited page are nearly all in English, so a per-language list would have too few cases; revisit when non-English answers are in.

Link text for capture (4 Nov 2026): the same signal, used to decide which links to open, not only to label what was harvested. Measured first with `anchor_capture_eval.py` on a spread sample of companies, because opening named links costs requests on every company site each night and a landing page can hold old or third-party PDFs. One hop only: a report microsite usually links its PDF from its first page, and a deeper crawl would follow navigation. The year in a link text names the document ("Sustainability Report 2025"), which is right for its label and title; the reporting year of the data inside comes from the period it covers, so a link text year never replaces `year` on its own. Rejected: making link text a trigger in `doc_links` straight away (every monitored page would open its named links before we know the yield), and following named links to other sites (suppliers, peers and standard setters are named like reports too). First sample (30 companies, 4 Nov 2026): no direct finds at all; 1,034 one-hop PDFs not held in 18 companies, but 70% of them in three companies' statutory and shareholder archives (Banorte, Acea, Inditex), privacy notices and training documents among them, and only 70 labelled 2023 or later. So a nightly rule, if any, keeps only key types and recent years, and the measure that decides it is that count, not the total. Full sample (150 companies, 5 Nov 2026): 400 key PDFs labelled 2023 or later in 37 companies (25%), three companies holding 23% of the finds, 21 direct finds in 5. Decided: a nightly rule (`one_hop.py`), one level, same site, only companies with a document gap or an overdue report, only those types and years, 300 rows a night and 10 a company, and a guard at 85% of the record limit. The label year only decides "recent enough"; the reporting year is read from the document like any other row. Rejected: every company every night (most finds in companies with no gap add rows without closing anything, with the base at 82%), and direct finds as a rule of their own (21 in 150 companies).

## Plausibility of proposed values: learned bounds, flags only (5 Nov 2026)
Proposals such as revenue 2.11 KRW (validated 2,068,192,612,476 KRW) and 2,043,127 for 2,043,127,000,000 reached the review table as plain proposals. Every company in the universe is large, so a value outside what the validated answers show is a reading error, most often a scale. `plausibility.py` learns each attribute's range from the validated answers (1st to 99th percentile, widened 5 times; currencies in US dollars at the ECB's daily rates), compares each proposal with the company's own validated other years (10 times either way; the first dry run used 4 times and other proposals as history, and flagged right values nearly as often as wrong ones: precision 54%), and checks revenue per employee against the validated pairs. A scale slip is named only against the company's own years, since companies differ a thousand times in size, and without a rescaled number: on the second dry run none of 7 rescaled values equalled the validated answer. Second dry run: 118 flags on rows with a validated answer, 75% of them wrong (zero 100%, range 80%, per head 79%, history 72%); recall 6%, so the flag catches the absurd values, not a wrong figure that looks plausible. Flags only: value and status never change, so no reader's score can get worse. Rejected: margins per attribute set by hand (they go stale and nobody owns them), and correcting values automatically (a suggestion is right only when the company's own years are right; measured first, see the run on #224).

## Six ways to choose the model reader's pages, measured before any is used (5 Nov 2026)
After the ranking experiment (query expansion 69% of cited pages in the top 3 against 44% for today's search) and the narrative diagnosis (finding explains few errors there), the next lever for the number attributes is which 8 pages the model reader sees. `select_eval.py` measures six ideas against today's choice on the same validated answers, with no model reading: query expansion, a learned document-type prior, a learned ranker (logistic regression over today's rank, rule candidate, type prior, expansion score, meaning, numbers and position), meaning by multilingual embeddings, translated words for documents not in English, and a model locate pass. Only the rank half of the 8 changes; the rule-candidate half stays as today, so a method cannot lose today's best rule pages. A method that qualifies (no attribute more than 3 points worse) is then run with the model reading, next to today's, before it becomes the default. Rejected: building a vector index of all 2 million pages first (embedding the 25 candidate pages of each answer on the runner measures the idea for nothing), and switching any method on from the ranking experiment alone (the narrative lesson of 5 Nov).

## Evidence packs instead of whole pages, measured first (5 Nov 2026)
The owner's review of narrative disagreements showed three things: the model's quotes are often too short to justify an answer, the evidence can sit on several pages and documents while the reader answers from one, and whole pages spend the model's reading on text unrelated to the attribute. ## Learned page choice ships as an option, trained once and kept in a file (5 Nov 2026)
Of the six page choice ideas measured on 402 validated answers (`select_eval.py`, 5 Nov), the learned ranker did best: the number on the 8 pages for 65% against 63% today, Revenue +5 and Employees +3 points, no attribute worse. Expansion did not qualify at full size (capex 4.5 points worse; on the first 48 answers it had looked 8 points better). Doctype, embeddings, translation and the locate pass qualified but added 0 to 0.5 points, so they are not built. The learned ranker becomes `extract_eval --select learned` (`page_select.py`), default base. Its weights, type prior and expansion words are trained once by `select_eval.py --save` and kept in `select_learned.json`, per attribute both without each company fold (for scoring validated answers: a company is never scored with what its own cited pages taught) and on all folds (for answers nobody has validated). The meaning feature needs sentence-transformers on the runner; a learned run without it stops instead of silently scoring a different method. Rejected: training inside every extract_eval run (40 minutes of database reading each time, and propose values would need the same), and a fallback without embeddings (not what was measured). Note the ceiling: only 67% of the numbers are on the 25 ranked pages at all, so page choice can add at most 4 points; the larger losses are earlier (documents and ranking) and in the reading.

## Year windows per attribute, learned from the analysts' citations (6 Nov 2026)
Both readers read the documents of the answer's document year and the next. That suits figures of the year (revenue, emissions) but not standing disclosures: a human rights policy published the year before still answers the question (the owner, 5 Nov 2026). The windows are only measured here; a reader option that uses them (with the newest document first, never one later than the year asked plus one) is built and run next to today's before it changes anything. Rejected: windows set by hand per attribute, and using the analysts' Source Year field directly (it is filled inconsistently; our library's year is the one the readers filter on, so that is what is measured, with Source Year kept only for sources not in our library).

## Choose the figure with a learned probability model, not a prompt (7 Nov 2026)
The owner's direction: more signals from mathematical and probabilistic models, not only better prompts. The numbers' losses are in the choice among printed figures (the right figure is on the model's pages for 66% to 72% of answers, right for 58% to 63%), and the rule reader makes that choice with hand-set weights. `chooser_eval.py` replaces them with a conditional logit (the standard model of discrete choice: the probability that the analyst chose a candidate is the softmax of a weighted sum of its features over the answer's candidates), fitted on the validated answers by company fold. It needs no model, so it can be measured on thousands of answers instead of 65 a run. If it beats the hand weights, the next step is the same chooser over the model reader's candidates (the model lists, the probabilities choose).

## Type size and weight as signals, measured (7 Nov 2026)
Google's early index kept, for every word, whether it was printed large, capitalised, or in a title or link ("fancy hits"). Our pages come from pypdf as plain text, so a label in a page heading and the same words in a footnote look alike. `chooser_eval --fancy` reads type size and the bold flag from the PDF for the kept pages only and adds them to the learned choice, reported next to the choice without them on the same answers and folds. If they carry weight, the same signals go into page ranking.

## Held-out attributes: the readers are measured on attributes nobody tuned them for (8 Nov 2026)

## The previous year's figure as an anchor, as an option (9 Nov 2026)
Reports print the previous year beside the current one ("Revenue 5,147 4,892"). When the previous year's value is known (validated, or in production accepted), searching THAT number in this year's documents lands on the table holding this year's figure, whatever the line is called and in whatever language. Company memory (1 Nov 2026) used the other year's line words, never its value; `--anchor pages` adds the pages printing the value to the pool with ANCHOR_WEIGHT 1.5 (`company_memory.anchor_pages`), `--anchor note` also tells the reader that the value is the other year's, printed as a comparative, and that the figure asked is the one beside it (never that value). Part of the "arithmetic of the report" idea the owner asked for on 9 Nov: a report is a system of numbers that repeat and reconcile, and no reader used that. Measured against the same answers with memory alone; the share of answers with an anchor found, with it on a page holding the figure, and right is reported per attribute. Rejected: passing the value as the memory hint by default (the memory design kept the value out so the reader cannot echo it; the note variant is measured on its own).

## How often a figure is printed, as a page signal (9 Nov 2026)
A group total is printed on several pages of a report (key figures, the statement, the notes, the chairman's letter); a segment line or a stray figure is printed once. No reader used that count. `--frequency pages` takes a census of the big numbers printed on two or more pages of the year's documents (`recurring_numbers`, one query per answer with its own 6 s limit, numbers compared by digits so 5,147 and 5.147 are one) and raises a page's score by FREQ_WEIGHT 0.7 times the share (up to 5) of its big numbers that recur (`frequency_boost`). It needs no labels, so it also serves attributes with no hand-written spec. The run also reports the diagnostic behind the idea: of the answers whose figure is in the documents, how many print it on 2+ and 3+ pages, and the reader's accuracy when the figure recurs against when it is printed once. Rejected for the first step: telling the reader each number's count in the prompt (a second variant, if the page signal shows the figure does recur), and choosing the most frequent candidate without a model (no-model choosers lost before).

## Quorum: the same pages read twice, as a confidence signal (9 Nov 2026)
Two identical runs disagree on 5.6% of answers and reversing the page order moves 7.8%, so a single read carries noise and no measure of its own confidence. `--reads N` reads the model's pages N times (reversed, then rotated, so each read starts from another page); the answer scored stays the first read's, so accuracy is unchanged by construction, and the run reports the share of answers where the reads agree, accuracy when they agree and when they do not, and the ceiling if the right read could always be chosen. The point is the signal: if agreement is right far more often than disagreement, disagreement is the trigger for the second level (a reader that navigates the document) and for an adaptive page budget, and agreement is where production can accept without review. Like quorum sensing in social insects: act when enough scouts agree. Costs N times the model calls, so it is measured first, not switched on.

## A document map, drawn once and searched by every attribute (9 Nov 2026)
Finding the page is where the number reader loses most, and the search runs on each attribute's hand-written labels; without them the figure was on the ranked pages for 18% of answers. An analyst navigates by the contents, not by words. `doc_map.py` writes, once per page of the key documents, one model line saying what the page holds (section or table, metrics, years) into `public.page_map`, with its words and a multilingual sentence embedding, and `map_pages` ranks a document's pages for any attribute from its name and definition (words scaled to the best, plus cosine, half and half). `extract_eval --select map` reads the map's pages, `map-half` half map and half today's; a company without a map reads as today, and the run reports the share with a map, how often the map's pages hold the figure, and accuracy with and without. Scope chosen with the owner: the key document types only (annual, sustainability and integrated reports, 10-K, financial statements, registration documents, codes of conduct and policies), companies with validated answers first (about 250,000 pages, about $20 at the Haiku list price), the rest of the library (about $300) only if the map earns it on held-out attributes. Rejected: summarising every document type (about 6.6 million pages); embeddings of the raw pages instead of lines (measured 5 Nov: +0 to 0.5 points; a page's first 1,500 characters are mostly boilerplate, a written line is not); a new vector store (pgvector is already in the schema).

## The spec writer learns from the hand-written specs (9 Nov 2026)
`--spec-by model` wrote labels from the definition alone. Since #290 its prompt carries four hand-written development specs (revenue, Scope 1, employees, low-carbon capex: name and definition, then the labels and exclusions that won) as worked examples, so the model sees how a definition becomes the telegraphic row labels reports print, in several languages, and what to exclude. The attribute being written never appears among the examples, so under `--auto-spec` a development attribute is written without its own spec, and held-out attributes still see no answer and no page: the examples are development material, allowed by the held-out rule. `--spec-examples off` keeps the definition-only writer for comparison. Suggested in an outside review the owner shared (few-shot from the winning specs; its second suggestion, checking each written label against the corpus and adding the terms found next to figures, is noted for later). Rejected: a hand-curated example set per attribute kind (one fixed set of four keeps the comparison simple; kinds can come later if the examples help).

## The yes/no reader may say "not found", as an option (9 Nov 2026)
On held-out yes/no attributes the reader (62%) did not beat always answering the more common answer (67%), and on one attribute it said no 17 times where the analyst said yes. The prompt made no room between "a passage says the company does not" and "no passage addresses it": both were "no". `--abstain on` (`pack_eval.ABSTAIN`) gives the reader a third answer, "not found", for passages that do not address the question. For the score it still counts as no, so accuracy is comparable with today's; the run reports the share not found, the analyst's yes among them (what a wider search, the second level, could recover) and the accuracy among found answers. In WBA's method an undisclosed item is a no, so the third answer is not a different final answer; it is the trigger for looking further (more passages, the company's policies) before saying no, and the measure of how much there is to recover. Rejected: scoring "not found" as abstention (right or wrong would then depend on a policy nobody has set); a prior from the attribute's base rate (it would beat the constant answer by construction and say nothing about the reader).

## Slot reading: the accounting variants asked one by one, the choice made in code (9 Nov 2026)
Total capex stays at about 41% because the reader finds a capital expenditure, not the analysts' one: 8 of 12 wrong proposals were within 15% of the validated figure. Asking the model for "the" figure makes it choose between legitimate variants (property, plant and equipment only or with intangibles; cash paid or additions accrued; the figure the report itself calls capex; guidance). `--slots on` (`model_reader.read_slots`) asks for every variant the pages print, as its own checked entry (`attributes.json` "slots": total capex 5 variants, low-carbon capex 5, total R&D 4, Scope 1+2 7 plus three sums computed in code, "slot_sums"); the proposal scored is the first variant present, and the run reports, per variant, how often the pages printed it and how often it equals the analyst's figure. That table is the analysts' definition read from their own answers: the variant most often equal is the one they use, which the owner's question to them on 10 Nov can confirm rather than discover. Once confirmed, the choice becomes a priority rule in code, never a longer note in the prompt (rules in the prompt cost market 16 points, 7 Nov). Suggested in an outside review the owner shared (multi-slot extraction with a deterministic resolver). Rejected: letting the model name the variant it chose (it did, implicitly, and was 15% off); computing low-carbon capex as a share times total capex before the analysts say they do that.

## The second level reads more pages, not a bigger model (9 Nov 2026)
Sonnet on the same pages gained 1.9 points (chance) while the right pages given gain 7 to 13, so a second level is worth building only if it reads further. `--escalate empty` has the same model read the next MODEL_PAGES ranked pages when the first read gives no figure; `--escalate disagree` also when the reads of `--reads N` disagree, the second level then voting between them (`escalation_choice`: it replaces the first read only when it agrees with another read). Only escalated answers pay for a second call, so the cost stays a fraction of reading everything. The run reports the share escalated, how often the next pages hold the figure, and the second level's figures and hits. Chosen over reading the whole document at the second level (the pool already holds the label-matching pages; the whole document comes when the map or the content index names a page outside the pool) and over a larger model (measured, no gain). Trigger conditions still to add once measured: a failed arithmetic check (parts against totals), a figure far from the company's memory.

## Navigation by the table of pages, and the adaptive page budget (9 Nov 2026)
Two more of the owner's seven ideas, as options. `--select navigate` (information scent, Pirolli and Card): a model reads the documents' map lines (one line per page, `doc_map.py`), not the pages, and names the pages most likely to print the figure; those are read. One short call per answer (a few thousand tokens), the way an analyst reads the contents before opening a page; `navigate-half` mixes them with today's. `--escalate-steps N` turns the second level into a ladder: with `--model-pages 8 --escalate empty --escalate-steps 2` the reader reads 8 pages, then 8 more only when empty, then 16 more, so most answers cost half of today's 16 pages and the hard ones read 32 (the marginal value theorem: leave the patch when the yield falls). Both are measured against the 16-page default on the same answers and on held-out attributes before any default changes.

## A reranker learned from the analysts' validated pages (9 Nov 2026)
The last of the owner's seven ideas: the validated answers say, through their figures, which page holds each attribute; `rerank_train.py` turns them into (attribute name and definition, page text, holds or not) pairs, with the pool's non-holding pages as hard negatives, and fine-tunes a small multilingual cross-encoder (`mmarco-mMiniLMv2-L12-H384-v1`). The query carries no labels, so the model can score a page for an attribute it never saw. `extract_eval --select rerank` trains it at the start of the run on the development attributes' answers after the first 100 per attribute (the evaluations read the first ones, so no answer is both trained and scored) and orders the pool with it before `pages_for_model`. Held-out attributes never enter the training set, so their score with `--select rerank` is the honest measure of generalisation. Chosen over the earlier learned ranker (logistic regression over rank features and sentence embeddings, +0 to 0.5 points, 5 Nov) because a cross-encoder reads the page against the attribute instead of comparing two fixed vectors. Rejected: training inside the gold companies' folds (five models per run); storing the model in the repository (about 120 MB).

## Consensus: how often the report repeats the proposed figure, as a confidence signal (9 Nov 2026)
The third piece of the "arithmetic of the report" idea: after the model proposes a figure, the run counts on how many pages and in how many of the year's documents that figure is printed (`consensus`, one indexed query per proposal), and reports accuracy for figures printed once against two or more pages, and in one against two or more documents. A diagnostic on every model run, not an option: if figures the report repeats are right far more often than figures printed once, "printed once" joins the triggers of the second level and "printed in two documents" is where production can accept without review.

## A year-on-year check with a learned bound, measured in the shadow first (9 Nov 2026)
The plausibility job flags 75% right but catches 6% of the wrong proposals: most wrong figures are a wrong line or a wrong column, within the attribute's range and within 10 times the company's other years. The owner asked for checks by magnitude and by progression between years. Magnitude is the range check already; progression is new: the proposal against the company's nearest validated year, as a change per year, flagged when it changes faster than the validated pairs of years of that attribute do. The bound is learned per attribute as the 99th percentile of the validated year-on-year ratios (companies with two or more validated years, 20 pairs at least), so revenue, which rarely doubles in a year, gets a tight bound and emissions, which halve after a divestment, a loose one; nothing is set by hand. The first shadow measure used the maximum instead and gave revenue a bound of 5.7e12 a year: one validated pair in the analysts' data has a unit slip, and one bad pair must never set a bound, which is also why the range check uses percentiles. It is an option (`--progression on`) and off by default: every run also computes it in the shadow and reports its own precision and the recall it would add, so the nightly measurement decides the switch, as the reading options do. Rejected: tightening HISTORY_RATIO (one number for every attribute; 4 times flagged right values as often as wrong on 5 Nov), and using the company's other proposals as the history (a wrong 2022 proposal flagged a right 2024 on the first dry run). First shadow measure with the percentile (9 Nov 2026, PR dry run): only revenue has enough pairs (239 after 3 slips are left out), its bound is 20.4 times a year, the check flags 14 rows with a validated answer, 10 of them wrong (71%), and adds no recall: every row it flags the history check (10 times) already flags, because 20 is looser than 10. So the learned bound is only as clean as the validated pairs it learns from, and today the check adds nothing; it stays off, measured nightly, until more attributes have two validated years. A tighter percentile chosen to make it flag would be hand tuning by another name.

## lanterN's pool of pages: one statement a level, bounded by pages read (10 Nov 2026)
A search took 3 to 10 seconds and was cut by the clock, so the same search answered differently under load (the owner got 3 results for a phrase where a second try gave 7, and none while the database was busy). `search_timing.py` (run 42838335878) timed the stages on six searches. The ranking of the pool (0.2 to 1.1 s) and the 50 excerpts (0.2 s) were not the cost. The pool was: `find_pages` asked the word index once per year (31 years) and per subset of words, and every statement costs about the same whatever it returns (0.2 to 1.3 s), because the index lists every page holding the words before the first row comes back. Year by year "Nike sustainability" took 24 s where one statement took 0.9 s; "just transition" 97 s against 0.2 s. `companies_matching` added 2 s to every search with a company (it read 287,000 documents for a name).
Decided: a level is one statement (level 1, any subset missing one word, is one or-ed query); with no company and no year the level is two statements, the last three years first (500 pages) and the rest (300), so a common word still fills the pool with recent pages; one year is asked of the index as an equality, a span of years is checked on the rows; company names come from `companies` (`find_company_ids`); relaxing to fewer words happens only under ten pages; nothing is cut by the clock. Measured from the published page afterwards: Nike sustainability 1.7 s (was 6.7), Unilever scope 1 2024 1.0 s (was 2.7), climate 1.9 s, oil and gas wba 1.5 s, a company alone 0.2 s (was 1.3 to 2.7).
In the same change, the ranking reads the document's type and title (a word asked that the document is about multiplies the score by up to 3; `type_words` gives a type its other names, so "sustainability" reaches an Impact, ESG or CSR report), and a page held twice shows once.
Rejected: a cache of results (does not fix the first search); keeping one statement per year for the most recent years only (three statements cost three times one).
Known limit, accepted for now: whether words stand together is not in the index; it is checked on each page's word list, about a millisecond a page from disk. A quoted phrase is therefore checked on at most 3,000 pages a statement, and the neighbouring-words pass on 1,500. A rare phrase made of two very common words alone ("ACT Core" in quotes) can find nothing in its sample (it found 3 to 7 pages before, by reading for as long as the clock allowed); with any narrowing word, company or year it is found ("ACT Core World Benchmarking Alliance": 22 pages, 2.7 s). The cure is an index of word pairs, which would fit the main reports from 2024 on (the semantic index's scope); not built.

## Exact phrases get an index of word pairs, built with the semantic index (10 Nov 2026)
After the pool redesign a quoted phrase of two very common words alone ("ACT Core") could find nothing: standing together is checked page by page, a millisecond each from disk, on a bounded sample. With the pilots (+3.9, none worse) the no-going-back rule is met for the number kind. What is left is not a quality question but a cost one: as the default in the nightly Propose values it multiplies the cost per doubtful answer, so the nightly cap covers fewer answers. The owner decides the cap; until then it stays an option.

## The whole document on doubtful answers becomes the default of the nightly reader (10 Nov 2026)
Both measurements met the no-going-back rule (pilots +3.9, held-out numbers +7.7, no attribute worse), so the question left was cost: about twelve times more per answer where most first reads are empty. The owner's decision: default on in the nightly Propose values, cap raised from 1.5 to 5 USD a night. `propose_values.py --escalate none` turns it off; `extract_eval.py` keeps `--escalate none` as its default so that comparisons stay explicit. To watch in the first nights: answers written per night against the cap, and the share of answers that escalate.

## Ask: an answer box over the search, three questions a day (10 Nov 2026)
The owner asked to open the model's answers now, like a web search's answer box, with a daily limit instead of waiting for a budget decision. Decided: the answer is written from the search's own best six pages only and cites them; it is signed (a WBA address) so that the limit is per person: three answered questions a day, failures not counted. About 5,000 input tokens and 150 output tokens a question with Haiku, well under a cent; at three a day a person the cost is bounded by the number of people.
Where the call is made was the real choice. The page is static and public, so the key cannot be there. Chosen: the database calls the model (the `http` extension inside the `ask` function) with the key in Supabase Vault, read by the function only; it ships with `search.sql` like every other function and needs no new deployment. Rejected: a Supabase Edge Function (a second deployment path and its own secrets to keep in step), and a GitHub workflow as a relay (minutes of delay). Costs of the choice: a database connection is held for the few seconds of the model's answer (15 connections, three questions a person a day), and the function's timeout is 75 seconds.
Limits known: the model sees the first 3,000 characters of each page, so a figure at the foot of a long page can be missed; the answer is only as good as the six pages the search ranks first; documents are data to the model (the prompt says so), but an answer must still be checked against its source, and the card says it.

## lanterN prefers recent pages, and whole matches over repetition (10 Nov 2026)
The owner's rule, for lanterN and beyond: a person searching wants the most recent results when there are any. "living wage Nike" showed a 2021 Impact Report page first, one that says "wage" eleven times and never "living wage", above recent pages. Two causes, both changed in `find_pages`: the age discount was mild (15% a year in the divisor, so five years cost 43%) and the text score grew with repetition faster than the bonuses for holding every word and for the words standing together could offset. Now the score falls by a quarter for each year of age (five years leave 24%), a document with no year counts as three years old (it counted as new), and the text score enters as its square root. A year typed in the box is a filter, so older documents are one word away.
Not measured yet: there is no set of judged searches for lanterN. The click log is the measure to build (a click on a result lower than the third, or a second search, says the order was wrong); until then the samples tried from the page are the evidence, listed in the pull request.

## Sources added in lanterN go straight to the inbox (10 Nov 2026)
The owner: the analyst who searches and does not find knows which document is missing, and should be able to give its link from the search, for one company or several links at once; and the links should enter directly, without a person approving them first. Decided: the page stores the links signed (`source_submissions`), refusing those whose address the library already holds; `source_submissions.py` pastes them hourly into the company's `add_link` box, the same door a person uses in Airtable, so `inbox.py` applies the harvester's duplicate guards and decides document or page by itself. Rejected: writing `report_library` rows from the page or the script (a second way in, without the guards), and a review step before the inbox (the inbox already refuses duplicates and says what it did in `add_result`; who added a link is kept). A link passed on is worth three points in the review levels. Limits: one company per form, and the hourly schedule can run late.

## The word-pair index enters the search as an option (11 Nov 2026)
What: `find_pages` asks `page_pairs` first when the option `pairs` is on (table `search_options`, or `set lantern.pairs = 'on'` in a session): the pages holding every pair asked, then the pages holding at least one; a quoted phrase is still checked on the page; when these fill the list asked for, the word index is not asked. Not used with a company filter (the word index answers that fast). Why an option: the pair index covers the main reports from 2024 on only, so with it on a search of two or three words alone no longer returns older years or other document types unless the pairs do not fill the list; that is a change in what is found, and the rule is to measure first (`search_compare.py`: seconds, pages with the words together in the first ten, overlap, and the pages people opened in past searches). Rejected: replacing the bounded phrase check outright (documents outside the pair index would lose phrase search); adding the year to the pair index (not needed while it holds three years).

## "Did you mean" from titles and company names (11 Nov 2026)
What: `search_vocab` holds the words of document titles (in 5 documents or more) and of company names, rebuilt when the search functions are installed. A word asked that is not there and that fewer than 200 pages hold is compared with the list by edit distance (1, or 2 for words of 8 letters or more); the page offers the corrected text, the search is not changed. Rejected: the vocabulary of every page (`ts_stat` over 11.9 million pages is hours); correcting silently (a rare word that is right would be replaced).

## The review game counts right answers on control cards (11 Nov 2026)
What: one card in five is a value whose validated answer is known; a right verdict there is 3 points, a wrong one takes 2 away, any other review stays 1 point (a quarter under four seconds). Only clear cases are control cards: the robot's figure within 0.5% of the validated one (right) or beyond double or half of it (a scale error, wrong). Why: points by volume could be earned by clicking; and the robot's figure and the validated one often differ for a good reason (a restatement, another basis), where a reviewer who reads the page well would say "right" and be punished, so the cases in between are not used. Rejected: every validated answer as a control (unfair in between); showing which cards are controls (they would be the only ones answered with care).

## Confidence in a value is a measured share (11 Nov 2026)
What: beside a value the panel says how often readings like it matched the validated answer, by attribute and evidence group, with the number of cases (`value_confidence`, groups under 20 cases fall back to the attribute, then to all). Why: "3 of 4 documents agree" describes the documents, not the chance of being right; the measured share does, and it shows where the reader is weak. Rejected: a score from the model (not calibrated); agreement alone (two documents repeat the same wrong figure).

## Archiving: periodic documents only, text verified, files copied first (11 Nov 2026)
The owner paused archiving on 9 Nov and set three conditions; the first two are in `archive_documents.py`, the third blocks `--commit`. A policy, a code, a certification or a statutory document has no reporting year that makes it old (the one dated 2019 may be the one in force), so they and untyped documents are never candidates, under either rule. A candidate must have its text in the evidence database, checked row by row, not assumed. The purge deletes the attached PDF, so each PDF must be copied to object storage before; that needs a bucket and its keys (owner's choice), and until it is built the script refuses to tick anything. Rejected: relying on the document's public address to get the file back (companies remove old reports; that is why the Wayback search exists).

## Ask turns a long question into search words first (11 Nov 2026)
A question written in full ("BP has set intensity targets for its lifecycle emissions (S1&S2 as well as several S3 categories ...). Is this true?") is not a search: no page holds those words, and searching all of them was also what took the database down that morning. ## A company does not need a WBA id (11 Nov 2026)
What: the companies table takes companies that WBA's list does not hold (chosen for next year's list and still without an id, or never covered). Each gets lanterN's own id, `LN_` and the first 8 hex digits of the SHA-1 of its LEI, or of its name without accents, case, dots and legal endings when it has no LEI; it is written in the same `wba_id` field every script already keys on. Why: the list is updated months after a company is chosen, and the owner wants the system to stand without it; the id is deterministic so a rerun or a second request never makes a second row. Entry by hand (rows in Airtable), or by request from the page, which a person approves before anything is searched. Rejected: a counter (`LN_00001`: two runs could give two ids to one company); waiting for the WBA id (the reason for the change); a new id field beside `wba_id` (every script and the evidence database key on `wba_id`; a WBA id given later is an alias to add, not a second key). Not built: replacing an `LN_` id by the WBA id when one is given (it renames the company's documents; to do once, with care).

## The archived PDFs go to the project's own storage (11 Nov 2026)
What: Supabase Storage, private bucket `archive`, one object a document at `<wba_id>/<library_id>.pdf`; a row is marked (`stored_file`) only after the stored size equals the bytes sent. Why this store: the project already pays for it, the first 100 GB are included (the rule B candidates are 55 GB), and the owner may carry the system on outside WBA, where one provider is simpler to keep than two. Rejected: Cloudflare R2 (cheaper beyond 100 GB, one more account and set of keys); GitHub Releases (2 GB a file is enough, but 55 GB of binaries in a public repository's releases is not what they are for). The ceiling of a file's size is the storage's own setting (50 MB by default): a larger file is counted and left in Airtable, its document not archived.

## The robot will say who it is; measured first (11 Nov 2026)

## Documents a site refuses to the robot are sent from a person's browser (11 Nov 2026)
What: a Chrome extension, Send to lanterN. On a page that lists reports it shows the PDF links, the person ticks them and chooses the company, and the browser downloads them and stores them in the evidence database's private bucket `uploads`; `browser_uploads.py` makes the library rows. Why: one retailer's site answered 403 to the robot from the cloud and from the office network for weeks, and the owner saved 26 annual reports by hand, one at a time. A person's browser is the one visitor such a site accepts. Why an extension and not a watched folder on the person's computer: the folder still needs every PDF saved by hand, and cannot take a page. Why the file goes to the database's storage and not straight to Airtable: the extension holds no secret (it carries the publishable key the search page carries, and the person signs in with the emailed code), and a storage rule admits only the object that `upload_begin` registered for that person. Rejected: giving the extension an Airtable token (a secret in a zip on a public site); fetching through the person's browser from a server (needs their computer on and a channel into it). ## A company added from the browser is a request, and its documents wait (11 Nov 2026)
What: in Send to lanterN a person can add a company that is not in the list. It becomes a row of `company_requests` (as "Missing a company?" does), a person ticks `approve` in Airtable, and `add_companies.py` gives the id. Documents sent before the approval are kept in the storage under `REQ_<request>` and follow the company once it exists. Why not create the company at once: the owner asked that no company ever be added twice, and the full comparison (LEI, name without legal endings, website host) lives in one place, `add_companies.decide`, against the companies table. The database refuses the obvious cases at once (same name, same website host) and chooses the existing company. ## The embeddings are indexed once, at the end of the fill (12 Nov 2026)
What: `public.chunks` has no index on its embeddings while `semantic_index.py` fills it; `--build-index` builds the HNSW index once every document is in. Why: a 330-minute run indexed about 245 documents, and a run that timed its writes spent 33 of 48 minutes writing the chunks, 8 embedding and 7 on the word pairs. Each insert into an HNSW index places its vector in the graph; building the graph once over the finished table is the documented faster way. Nothing reads the embeddings yet (the search by meaning waits for the query side), so the fill loses nothing by having no index. Rejected: a larger database machine (it pays every hour for a cost that is in the method); filling without the word pairs first (they were 7 minutes of 48, not the brake; this was assumed for a day before it was measured). The lesson is in the order: measure the parts of a slow job before choosing what to drop.

## The word pairs are indexed at the end too (12 Nov 2026)
What: `page_pairs` loses its GIN index for the fill, as `chunks` lost its HNSW index, and `--build-index` builds both. Why: once the first was gone, a 45-minute run spent 28 minutes embedding, 14 writing the pairs and 1 writing the chunks. Cost while it lasts: the search option `pairs` would scan the table without its index, so it stays off until the build; it was off already and nothing else reads the table. Not done: more runners in parallel.
